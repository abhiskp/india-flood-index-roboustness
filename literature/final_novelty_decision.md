# Final Novelty Decision

## 1. Closest Previous Work

**Tellman et al. (2020)** is the strongest conceptual overlap. It validates social vulnerability indicators with US flood deaths and property damage, compares individual socioeconomic variables, a composite SoVI measure and flood-intensity-only models, and concludes that hazard- and outcome-specific models can outperform a generic vulnerability index. This directly overlaps with our construct-specific validation logic and the question of whether generic composite indices are enough. It differs because it is US county/event regression, not India district flood prioritisation, and no later non-overlapping temporal holdout, GFD-style satellite comparison or state-block uncertainty was verified from available text.

**Bakkensen et al. (2017)** is the closest broader disaster-index validation study. It validates five resilience/vulnerability indices against observed losses, fatalities and disaster declarations in the southeastern United States. This strongly overlaps with external outcome validation of indices, but not with India-specific flood district ranking, satellite physical inundation validation or strict temporal holdout.

**Aroca-Jimenez et al. (2022)** is close on composite flood-vulnerability index validation and sensitivity. It constructs an integrated multidimensional vulnerability index and validates it through uncertainty/sensitivity analysis. It does not appear to test future human outcomes, satellite-observed inundation or simple historical baselines.

**Moreira et al. (2021)** is the most important review for positioning. It establishes that validation, sensitivity/uncertainty analysis and temporal dynamics are known gaps in flood vulnerability index research. This means our paper cannot claim that nobody has noticed validation gaps; the novelty must be narrower and empirical.

**Saharia et al. (2025)** is the original India DFSI paper. It creates the index we audit, but from available text it does not independently validate DFSI against satellite inundation, later fatalities, simple baselines or geographic holdouts.

## 2. What Is Already Established

- Composite flood vulnerability and severity indices are common.
- Sensitivity and uncertainty analysis of composite indicators is established methodology.
- External validation of vulnerability/resilience indices against disaster outcomes is already established.
- Flood social-vulnerability components have been validated against observed flood deaths and damages in the United States.
- Satellite flood mapping and the Global Flood Database are already established independent physical-inundation evidence.
- Reviews have already identified validation, uncertainty and temporal-dynamics gaps in flood-vulnerability-index literature.

## 3. What Appears Different In Our Study

The potentially novel part is the **combination** of features rather than any single ingredient:

- national India district-scale flood prioritisation centred on the published DFSI;
- independent satellite comparison for physical inundation using GFD;
- strict non-overlapping temporal validation for later recorded fatalities;
- comparison of complex indices/models against simple historical baselines such as past flood-event count;
- state-block uncertainty analysis;
- explicit test of whether added index/model complexity improves objective-specific district rankings.

No screened paper has yet been found that performs this full combination.

## 4. Strongest Defensible Novelty Statement

This study provides a leakage-aware validation of India district flood-priority rankings showing that, for distinct decision objectives, complex composite indices and fitted models do not automatically outperform simple historical indicators: physical-hazard measures align better with satellite-observed inundation, while future recorded flood-fatality burden is predicted about as well by past flood-event frequency as by a fitted historical-impact model.

## 5. Claims That Are Too Broad

- Do not claim to be the first study to validate vulnerability or disaster-risk indices against observed outcomes.
- Do not claim to be the first study to use satellite flood observations.
- Do not claim to be the first study to perform composite-index sensitivity analysis.
- Do not claim that DFSI is invalid.
- Do not claim that the fitted future-fatality model is clearly superior to simple baselines.
- Do not claim that one universal ranking works for physical inundation and future human consequences.
- Do not claim that historical event-volume changes represent physical flood trends without reporting caveats.

## 6. Reviewer's "So What?" Answer

Flood-priority rankings are often used as if a single composite score can guide many decisions. The close literature shows that validation gaps are known, but decision makers still need to know whether complex rankings add value for a specific objective. Our result matters because it shows that the answer can be no: a simpler indicator can match or outperform a fitted model for later fatality ranking, while a separate physical-hazard measure is more appropriate for satellite-observed inundation. The practical implication is not to abandon indices, but to validate them against the decision they are supposed to support and compare them with simple baselines.

## 7. Final Recommendation

**SHORT METHODOLOGICAL ARTICLE**

The project is novel enough for a focused methodological paper if framed narrowly around leakage-aware validation, simple baselines and construct separation in India district flood prioritisation. It is not strong enough for a broad full research article claiming a new superior index or model. A research note is also possible, but the combination of DFSI reproduction, satellite validation, temporal holdout and state-block uncertainty likely supports a short methodological article if the literature review remains consistent after full-text retrieval.
