# Literature Gap Summary

## 1. How flood-priority indices are normally constructed

Verified seed papers show that flood vulnerability and resilience indices commonly aggregate multiple indicators across exposure, susceptibility/sensitivity, resilience/adaptive capacity, social, economic, physical, environmental and institutional dimensions. Evidence from Moreira et al. (2021) indicates that min-max normalization, equal weighting and linear aggregation are common in flood vulnerability indices. India-specific district examples include Bihar, Uttarakhand and the national DFSI.

## 2. How weights are selected

Verified evidence shows several weighting approaches: equal weighting, PCA/statistical weighting, expert/AHP approaches, entropy-like approaches in some flood-damage work, and fixed formula weights in DFSI. Composite-indicator methodology papers warn that nominal weights may not equal realized importance and that weighting/aggregation decisions affect rankings.

## 3. How rankings are validated

Verified reviews report validation as underdeveloped. Moreira et al. (2021) found validation in only a small share of reviewed flood vulnerability index studies. Hamidi et al. (2025) similarly reports insufficient validation and ground-truthing in social vulnerability to floods. Bakkensen et al. (2017) and Tellman et al. (2020) are important examples of using observed disaster/flood outcomes to validate vulnerability/resilience indices or components.

## 4. Use of later outcomes

Evidence not yet sufficient. Verified seed papers include observed-outcome validation, but strict non-overlapping temporal validation of district flood-priority rankings remains unconfirmed from the seed set.

## 5. Use of simple baseline comparisons

Evidence not yet sufficient. Some verified work compares alternative indices or model components, but the seed set has not yet established routine comparison against simple historical indicators such as past flood-event count.

## 6. Use of satellite-observed inundation

Tellman et al. (2021) establishes the Global Flood Database as an independent satellite-observed inundation dataset. Moreira et al. (2021) reports that some flood-vulnerability index validation used flooded-area maps, but evidence is not yet sufficient on direct satellite validation of district-level composite flood-priority rankings in India.

## 7. Geographic validation

Evidence not yet sufficient. Verified flood-risk validation literature calls for better benchmark solutions and validation practice, but the current seed set has not yet established common state-block or geographic-holdout validation for flood-priority rankings.

## 8. Reporting changes in historical disaster data

Gall et al. (2009) supports the general concern that hazard loss databases contain biases and limitations. Jonkman and Kelman (2005) supports caution in comparing flood-death information. Evidence specific to India Flood Inventory reporting drift needs additional literature and dataset documentation.

## 9. What previous studies already establish

- Flood vulnerability and resilience indices are common and diverse.
- Weighting, aggregation, normalization and indicator selection strongly shape composite indicators.
- Validation and uncertainty analysis are widely recognized gaps.
- Observed disaster outcomes can be used to validate vulnerability/resilience indices.
- Satellite inundation can provide independent physical flood evidence.

## 10. What appears unresolved

- Whether complex district flood-priority rankings outperform simple objective-specific historical indicators.
- Whether published district flood indices are validated against later, non-overlapping outcomes.
- Whether physical inundation, exposure and human consequences should be separated more often in operational rankings.
- How reporting drift in historical disaster inventories affects temporal validation claims.

## 11. Evidence supporting our novelty

The strongest current novelty support is the combination of: national India district scale; reproduction of the published DFSI; external satellite convergent-validity testing; comparison with simple baselines; strict leakage-free temporal holdout for recorded future fatalities; and state-block uncertainty checks. Verified literature supports each component as important, but no verified seed paper yet matches this exact combination.

## 12. Evidence weakening our novelty

Bakkensen et al. (2017) and Tellman et al. (2020) strongly overlap in spirit because they validate disaster or flood vulnerability/resilience indices against observed outcomes. Moreira et al. (2021) and Hamidi et al. (2025) already identify validation gaps. Therefore the novelty cannot be claimed as simply "validating flood vulnerability indices"; it must be narrower and India/DFSI/temporal-baseline specific.

## 13. Remaining Literature Needed

- Direct papers on prospective or non-overlapping temporal validation of flood-risk/flood-priority indices.
- Papers comparing complex disaster-risk rankings with simple baselines.
- India-specific work on flood mortality prediction and district prioritisation.
- Studies on administrative boundary changes and disaster-inventory reporting bias in India.
- Spatial/geographic holdout validation studies in flood susceptibility, vulnerability or impact ranking.

## 14. Provisional Publication Judgement

Evidence not yet sufficient for a broad novelty claim. Current evidence supports a narrower methodological contribution if further screening confirms that strict temporal holdout plus simple-baseline comparison is rare in district flood-priority studies, especially for India.
